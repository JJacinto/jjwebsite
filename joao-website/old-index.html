Redefining webinar customization

As a product designer for StreamYard On-Air webinars, I led the design of webinar customization, enhancing the capabilities and flexibility of the tool for organizers customizing their webinars.

Core achievements

  • 1 out of every 3 webinars created used custom registration fields, 4 months after release
  • 3X the number of weekly webinars created since releasing webinar customization

Role

Senior / Staff Product Designer

Duration

4 months

Team

Lead designer, product manager, engineering manager, engineers, director of product

Responsibilities

Lead designer, researcher, front-end code reviewer

Competitive benchmarking Discovery Product design Research studies Prototyping Design system

Discovery — Uncovering the problem

Through research involving sales, support teams, user interviews, and concept tests, we identified a critical gap in the market. Hosts lacked customization options to maintain their brand identity, and couldn't collect registrants' data in a way that fit their unique needs.

We conducted tech reviews to assess feasibility and validate that our proposed solutions were technically viable within our platform constraints.

From discovery to definition — Finding the right solution

We set clear goals and developed a hypothesis based on our research findings. Our market research revealed that competitors offered extensive customization capabilities, validating the market need.

We explored multiple solution directions through ideation sessions and internal critiques. Prototype testing with users and internal stakeholders helped us refine our approach before committing to full development.

Step 1 - Webinar branding

Scalability vs feasibility: WYSIWYG vs minified previews

We faced a key design decision: should we build a full WYSIWYG editor (more powerful but resource-intensive) or a modal-based customization approach (simpler but limited)?

After evaluating both options against our technical constraints and user needs, we opted to launch v1 with minified previews—providing enough customization power while maintaining performance and development efficiency.

Step 2 - Custom registration fields

From a modal to a full page editor

After tracking usage data, we discovered 15% of webinars used branding in the first 3 weeks—a strong signal to invest further.

For registration fields, we designed a system supporting 5 field types in v1: phone number, country, custom text-field, custom checkbox, and custom single-select. Instead of a modal approach, we pitched and implemented a full-page editor to give users more space and reduce cognitive load.

The key innovation was designing a modular, multi-page registration system that could scale with future field types without redesigning the core interaction.

Understanding webinar organizers

As the lead designer for On-Air webinars, I planned, prepared and co-led a research study aimed at informing the future of our product.

Core achievements

  • Ran the company's first major study to understand webinar organizers
  • Generated critical insights to inform the product's roadmap, and briefed 2 other pods

Role

Staff Product Designer / Research co-lead

Duration

3 months

Team

1 lead designer (myself), 1 lead researcher

Responsibilities

Lead designer, co-researcher

User interviews Research methodology Participant recruitment Concept testing Insights synthesis Journey mapping

The challenge — Inform the future of our product

One year after launching On-Air, we lacked a long-term product vision. Our roadmap was primarily informed by sales calls, support requests, townhalls, and competitive benchmarks—all valuable but fragmented sources.

We needed to take a step back and deeply understand our core users: webinar organizers. What were their goals? Pain points? How did they think about webinars?

Deep-dive with webinar organizers

We recruited 11 participants across diverse industries—healthcare, education, and corporate communications—using the User Interviews platform. This diversity ensured we captured varied use cases and perspectives.

Through in-depth interviews and user scenario testing with concept prototypes, we explored how organizers approached webinar planning, execution, and post-event activities.

From notes to insights

We transcribed all interviews using Dovetail, then systematically aggregated findings into insight areas:

  • Product-market fit perceptions
  • Engagement needs and expectations
  • Post-webinar needs (repurposing, sharing)
  • Competitor impressions and gaps

This structured approach ensured we moved beyond surface-level observations to deeper, actionable patterns.

From insights into journey map

We synthesized the four insight areas into a comprehensive journey map covering five key phases:

  • Planning
  • Promotion
  • Execution
  • Analysis
  • Repurposing / Sharing

This journey map was presented to the entire company, creating a shared understanding of the user experience across all stakeholders.

Design Leadership

Leading and managing a design system

Implemented and led a cross-functional design system team across 6 teams for enhanced product quality, accessibility, and efficiency.

Core achievements

  • Coordinated 6 product teams into a unified design system effort
  • Enabled improved accessibility (WCAG AA) and refined components
  • Laid foundations for dark mode implementation

Role

Lead designer and design system manager

Duration

10 months

Team

2 design leads, 6 designers, 7 engineers from 6 teams, 1 engineering manager

Responsibilities

Lead designer, Design system manager, Co-project manager, Front-end code reviewer

Design system Component library Accessibility Design tokens Cross-team collaboration WCAG compliance

The challenge — Design vs code disparity

We had a fragmented component landscape: 5+ variations of single components existed across different parts of the product. There was no shared Figma infrastructure, making collaboration between design and engineering inefficient.

Our design system work was ad-hoc and scattered, with no clear ownership or contribution model.

Organizing the team

We established clear OKRs for the design system initiative and held contribution model workshops to align all 6 teams on processes and standards.

This alignment was critical—without buy-in from the individual product teams, adoption would be difficult.

Establishing weekly practice

We instituted weekly cross-team sync meetings where designers and engineers from all 6 product teams collaborated on design system work.

We adopted atomic design principles and a React component library as our technical foundation, ensuring consistency from atoms to organisms.

Design — Tokens and scoped variables for light/dark mode

We implemented a token-based design system architecture with scoped variables. This allowed us to support both light and dark modes without duplicating components.

The token system became the single source of truth for colors, spacing, typography, and other design properties across all products.

Reshaping an AI training platform

A leader in Contact-centre-as-a-service software, Talkdesk challenged me as a designer to design for AI Trainer product, co-leading discovery and delivery to improve AI trainer usability.

Core achievements

  • Co-led discovery and delivery to improve AI trainer product
  • Demystified complex user tasks into a more comprehensive and intuitive experience

Role

UX Designer

Duration

11 months

Team

1 lead designer, 6 designers, 7 engineers, 1 engineering manager, 1 product manager

Responsibilities

Lead designer

AI/ML Discovery User research Prototyping User testing Complex workflows

The challenge — Content validation wasn't being used

The AI Trainer product had a content validation feature that users weren't adopting. Research indicated users didn't understand how to train AI models, and the feature felt disconnected from their workflows.

We needed to redesign the experience to make AI model training more intuitive and integrated into the natural user journey.

Initial audit and research

We conducted discovery sessions, mapped information architecture and user journeys, and shadowed users in their actual environment. Interviews revealed mental model misalignments and feature discoverability issues.

What we discovered

Product adoption wasn't ideal because the labeling system wasn't being used as expected. Users didn't see the connection between collecting training data (labeling) and model improvement. The interface didn't guide them through the mental steps required to train an AI model effectively.

From low-fi to high-fi to user testing

We created wireframes to explore different interaction models, then developed high-fidelity prototypes. We conducted user testing across 3 iterations, learning and refining based on real user feedback at each stage.

Design — Simplifying complex concepts

We introduced intent proximity matching and model clustering ideation to simplify how users understand and organize training data.

The key was making abstract AI concepts tangible: rather than asking users to "label for training," we showed them how their actions directly improved the model's performance.

Designing a support chat experience

One of the biggest domain providers in the world, driven to pursue an amazing customer experience, challenged me to build an in-house customer support app.

Core achievements

  • Designed CS app and widget from scratch with own design system
  • Achieved 97.4% positive feedback rate 4 months after release

Role

UX Designer

Duration

10 months

Team

Lead designer, 7 engineers, 1 PM, 1 EM, 8 support engineers

Responsibilities

Lead designer, front-end code reviewer

Customer support Design system User research Accessibility Multi-channel White-label

The challenge — Build an in-house CS app

Namecheap was using Kayako for customer support, but it didn't fit their specific needs. They wanted to build an in-house customer support app with multichannel capabilities, replacing Kayako entirely.

The solution needed to serve both support agents (internal) and customers (external) across multiple channels.

Kickoff and research

We traveled to Namecheap's Ukraine HQ to shadow support agents and conduct interviews. This immersion was critical—we needed to understand their actual workflows, not theoretical ones.

We created an experience canvas and conducted competitive benchmarking to inform our approach.

What we discovered

Support agents had inefficient shift management and were juggling 20+ tools and 5+ simultaneous chats. Customers had difficulty accessing and using the chat feature—discoverability and accessibility were major issues.

The current system created friction at every touchpoint, from agent management to customer access.

Goal: Build an integrated solution

We committed to building a comprehensive platform that unified agent management, multi-channel communication, and customer access into a single, cohesive experience.

Low-fi, High-fi, Unmoderated tests

We created wireframes to explore interaction patterns, then high-fidelity system blueprints and user flows. Unmoderated testing with both agents and customers validated our assumptions before development.

Building a design system

We created a white-label design system supporting 2 themes and multiple component variants. Web accessibility (WCAG) was a core requirement, ensuring the platform was usable for all agents and customers.

The modular system allowed Namecheap to customize the experience while maintaining consistency across touchpoints.
